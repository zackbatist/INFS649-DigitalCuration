About me

I’m a postdoc in epidemiology here at McGill, where I work on the Covid-19 Immunity Task Force Databank.

The Databank brings together data from the studies the Task Force funded during the pandemic and makes them available to other researchers.

My own research there looks at how that sharing actually gets done, including the agreements, the coordination and the administrative work that let data move from one team to another.

Before that, I did my PhD at the Faculty of Information at the University of Toronto.

I studied how archaeologists work with data together, especially in large projects where many people record and interpret the same material.

I’ve also been involved in open source communities for a long time, and I help maintain a few community projects for archaeologists.

So I come to open science as someone who studies it, and also as someone who does a lot of its less visible work.

1 Open science as a movement

First, what open science is, and how it reaches researchers here in Canada.

NEXT SLIDE

UNESCO’s definition

UNESCO adopted a Recommendation on Open Science in 2021, and its definition is the one people quote most often.

The definition makes two commitments.

The first commitment is to access.

Research from every field should be available to everyone, for the benefit of scientists and of society.

The second commitment concerns how knowledge gets produced.

UNESCO asks that research itself be inclusive, equitable and sustainable, which raises questions about who gets to take part in research and on what terms.

Most open science policy and infrastructure addresses the first commitment.

The second gets far less attention.

NEXT SLIDE

What open science includes

Open science brings together several movements that grew up somewhat separately.

This figure divides them into six strands.

It’s one classification among several.

Benedikt Fecher and Sascha Friesike, for example, sort open science into five schools of thought according to what each one is trying to achieve, such as public participation in research or more efficient research.

Open access makes publications free to read.

Open data makes the data behind those publications available, and open source does the same for the software and code that process and analyse the data.

Open methods documents research protocols, so readers can see how a study was designed and carried out.

These four work as a unit.

A paper reports findings, and a reader who wants to evaluate those findings needs the data, the code and the protocol that produced them.

Open peer review extends the same transparency to evaluation.

Journals publish the reviews alongside the paper, and sometimes the reviewers’ names, so readers can see how the work was assessed and what changed in response.

Open educational resources apply open licensing to teaching materials, including textbooks and course notes.

They also draw on the other five, since openly licensed papers and datasets make good teaching material.

NEXT SLIDE

Justifications for open science

There are two kinds of justification for open science.

The first is epistemic.

When the data, code and protocols behind a paper are available, reviewers and other researchers in the field can rerun the analysis and test whether the reported results hold.

Errors that would otherwise go unnoticed can be caught and corrected after publication.

Shared data can also be combined with other datasets or reanalysed with different methods, which extends their value well beyond the project that produced them.

CLICK

The second is political and economic.

Public money funds most research, so the public should be able to read the results.

Openness also gives funders a clearer view of what their grants produce, which informs how they assess public investment in research and where they direct future funding.

And journal subscription costs have strained library budgets for decades, which is a big reason librarians were early advocates of open access.

Both kinds of justification concern research outputs, meaning the papers, datasets and code that can be released and counted.

NEXT SLIDE

Tri-Agency policies

In Canada, the three federal granting agencies, CIHR, NSERC and SSHRC, set these requirements together as the Tri-Agency.

On open access, the 2015 policy asked that articles be freely available within twelve months of publication.

A revised policy removes that delay.

For grants awarded from January 2027, articles have to be available in a Canadian repository or on a Canadian platform on the day they’re published.

On data, the Research Data Management Policy has come in stages.

Some funding calls have required a data management plan since 2022, and universities had to post institutional data management strategies in 2023.

The last stage is a deposit requirement.

Researchers would deposit the data, code and documentation behind their publications, and keep them for ten years.

It’s expected to start in 2027, but the agencies haven’t published the final text yet.

Each stage adds tasks for researchers.

A data management plan has to be written before any data exist, and a deposit has to be documented well enough for someone outside the project to use it.

NEXT SLIDE

Infrastructure behind the Tri-Agency policies

Researchers meet these policies through a set of national services, and each one supports a different requirement.

The Digital Research Alliance of Canada is a federally funded organization that coordinates national computing, storage and research data management.

It runs the DMP Assistant, the tool most Canadian researchers use to write the data management plans the Tri-Agency now asks for.

When a project has data to deposit, Borealis is a likely destination.

It’s a shared repository built on Dataverse, an open source platform for sharing and citing research data, and university libraries across the country run it together.

McGill has its own collection there.

Deposited data still have to be found.

The Alliance also runs the Federated Research Data Repository, which gathers metadata from Borealis and other Canadian repositories so that people can search them in one place.

All of these services depend on continued public funding and on the staff who maintain them.

2 Curation in open science

Every open output, whether a paper, a dataset or a piece of code, has to be described, deposited and kept usable before anyone else can rely on it.

That work is digital curation.

NEXT SLIDE

A paper and its linked records

This is what open science looks like around a single paper today.

The paper sits in the middle.

It has a DOI registered through Crossref, and its licence is recorded with that DOI.

On the left are the people and organizations behind it.

Each author has an ORCID identifier, and each institution has a ROR identifier.

The CRediT taxonomy describes what each author contributed.

Funders and grants carry identifiers too, and open reviews from services like eLife, PubPeer and Peer Community In link back to the paper.

On the right is what the paper rests on.

A registered plan records what the researchers meant to do before they collected any data.

The dataset has its own DOI from DataCite and sits in a repository like Zenodo, OSF or Borealis.

The code lives on GitHub or a similar platform, sometimes packaged as notebooks so that others can rerun the analysis.

OpenAlex gathers all these records, and the links between them, into one open catalogue.

Every one of these links exists because someone created it, checked it and keeps it current.

When an identifier is missing or wrong, the link breaks, and nobody downstream can follow it.

NEXT SLIDE

Decisions behind the records

Every record in that picture rests on decisions that someone has to make.

Someone decides how to describe the data so that people outside the project can find and interpret them, and what to record about how the data were made.

Someone decides how the project will handle data before it collects any, which licence to apply, and which repository to use.

Someone decides what to keep and for how long, how to show the funder the policy was met, and what happens to the data after the grant ends.

CLICK

Under the Tri-Agency policies, most of these decisions fall to researchers.

Archivists, librarians and data curators train for this work in information studies programs like this one.

Most researchers pick it up on the job, the same way they learn to teach or to manage a project, with little formal training and a lot of improvisation.

NEXT SLIDE

Who curates research data?

Digital curation has its roots in archives and libraries, where trained professionals take custody of material and care for it over time.

Costis Dallas, who supervised my doctorate, argues that this custodial view no longer covers what curation is.

Researchers, communities and users also curate, wherever they work with digital material that keeps changing.

Open science policy turns that observation into an obligation, since researchers now have to prepare their own data for others.

CLICK

The question is whether they have what they need to do it.

Ixchel Faniel and Elizabeth Yakel compared data sharing in three disciplines, and they found that the people who produce data often lack the money, tools and staff to prepare it for sharing.

In disciplines where repository or museum staff help, producers carry less of the load.

Elsewhere, the work stays with whoever is left in the project.

CLICK

Even professional curation is harder than it looks from outside.

Andrea Thomer and her colleagues studied curators at ICPSR, a large social science data archive.

The curators relied on judgement and constant back and forth with depositors, and their work rarely followed the steps that lifecycle models describe.

Much of it never appeared in any record of who contributed to the data.

So open science policy has moved skilled and largely invisible work onto researchers, many of whom have neither the training nor the support to do it well.

3 Complicating openness

Open science policy measures openness at the moment of release, when a paper is published or a dataset is deposited.

Curation is concerned with everything after that moment, for as long as anyone uses the data.

The difference shows up in how data carry context, in how access terms get set, in what reuse actually involves, and in who maintains the infrastructure.

NEXT SLIDE

Data and their context

Data get their meaning from how they were made.

That includes who collected them, with what instruments, under what conditions and for what purpose.

CLICK

Metadata are supposed to capture that context, and they do capture some of it.

The rest stays with the people who did the work.

It’s tacit knowledge, and they rarely think to write it down because to them it’s obvious.

Paul Edwards and his colleagues call this science friction.

In their study of interdisciplinary environmental research, metadata were never finished.

Making sense of shared data took ongoing conversation between the people who made the data and the people trying to use them.

Irene Pasquetto, Christine Borgman and Morgan Wofford found a similar pattern among scientists who reuse data.

For simple comparisons, reusers could get by with what had been deposited.

When they wanted to integrate someone else’s data into their own analysis, they generally preferred to work with the people who created them.

So documenting data is an act of interpretation.

Whoever does it, a curator or a researcher acting as one, has to anticipate what future users will need to know without knowing who those users are.

NEXT SLIDE

How open should these data be?

Here are four kinds of data.

For each one, decide whether it should be open, shared under controlled access, or kept closed.

Controlled access means approved users can get the data after they sign an agreement.

Closed means the data stay with the project team.

Run the vote, one row at a time

Air quality readings tend to go open with little disagreement.

Nobody is identifiable in them, and wide reuse is the point of collecting them.

Survey responses split more, because even anonymized answers can identify people once someone links them with other datasets.

Archaeological site locations usually end up controlled or closed, since publishing them invites looting.

Genomic data from an Indigenous community raise a different question.

Here the question is who has the authority to decide.

The answers changed with the data, and with the people the data describe.

Running the vote. Allow about five minutes.

  1. Read the two definitions at the bottom of the slide before starting.
  2. Go row by row. Ask for hands for open, then controlled access, then closed, one vote per person. Say the leading answer aloud.
  3. On the row with the most even split, ask one person from each side to explain, about thirty seconds each.
  4. Expect air quality readings to go open, survey responses to split between open and controlled access, and site locations to go controlled or closed. If site locations go open, raise looting. If survey responses go open, raise reidentification through linked datasets.
  5. For the genomic data, ask who should get to decide. Take one or two answers and move on, since the next slide gives the CARE principles’ answer.

As open as possible, as closed as necessary

The European Commission put the principle this way: as open as possible, as closed as necessary.

Openness is the default, and restriction is legitimate when there’s a reason for it.

Somebody has to weigh those reasons for each dataset, and then write the outcome into the record so the next person can follow it.

The FAIR principles are the best-known guide for that work.

They ask that data be findable, accessible, interoperable and reusable.

They’re about the data, and they say nothing about whose data these are.

CLICK

The CARE principles, from the Global Indigenous Data Alliance, address that gap.

CARE stands for collective benefit, authority to control, responsibility and ethics.

The principles assert the rights of Indigenous peoples over data about them, their lands and their knowledge, and they’re meant to sit alongside FAIR.

Controlled access sits between open and closed, and it takes a lot of work.

At the CITF Databank, researchers apply for access, a committee reviews each request, and approved users sign an agreement before they receive anything.

None of that shows up when we simply say the data are available.

NEXT SLIDE

Reusing data

Policies treat availability as the goal.

Once data are deposited, they count as open.

But reusers still have to find the data, decide whether to trust them and work out how to read them.

Ixchel Faniel and Elizabeth Yakel interviewed reusers in archaeology, quantitative social science and zoology.

Reusers drew on many sources at once.

They read the original publications, repository records, documentation and codebooks.

They talked to the people who produced the data, and sometimes they went back to the physical specimens or artifacts.

Above all, they judged data by how complete the documentation was.

CLICK

The weakest form of availability is the promise to share on request.

One team found nearly eighteen hundred articles whose authors said they would share their data on request, and then actually asked.

Fewer than one in seven authors responded, and 122 sent the data.

That’s under seven percent.

Each of these depends on curation, from describing data so that people can find them to documenting how they were made so that people can trust them.

NEXT SLIDE

Maintaining repositories

A deposit is a promise about the future.

Somebody has to keep the files readable, the links working and the servers running for years after the project ends.

The proposed Tri-Agency deposit requirement would have researchers retain their data, code and documentation for ten years.

The TRUST principles describe what a repository should be able to demonstrate before anyone hands it that responsibility.

Sustainability matters most here.

A repository has to show that it can keep operating and preserving its holdings over the long term.

CoreTrustSeal certifies repositories against requirements along these lines.

CLICK

Grants last a few years.

Storage, staff and format migrations cost money every year.

So who pays for years three through ten?

Often nobody planned for it, and the cost lands on a library or a repository that was never part of the original budget.

NEXT SLIDE

Release and upkeep

Context, access terms, reuse and maintenance all show the same pattern.

Open science asks for release.

Curation asks who looks after the data, for whom and for how long.

Those questions have no single answer, and the people answering them day to day barely appear in open science policy.

NEXT SLIDE

4 Methodological pluralism

So far I’ve talked about data as if all research produced the same kind.

It doesn’t, and that creates problems for a single model of open science.

NEXT SLIDE

Open science practices and confirmatory research

The standard open science practices grew out of one kind of research, the confirmatory experiment, and especially out of the replication debates in psychology and biomedicine.

Registering a study in advance means fixing the hypothesis and the analysis plan before collecting any data.

Replication means that someone else repeats the study to see whether the results hold.

Data sharing assumes that data can be detached from the people who made them and still make sense.

These practices suit some research well and other research badly.

Think of an ethnographer, an archaeologist excavating a site that the excavation itself destroys, or a field biologist recording a season that will never recur.

None of them can fix a hypothesis in advance in any meaningful way, and none of their studies can be repeated.

CLICK

Sabina Leonelli argues that standardized, top-down open science policy ignores this diversity.

Policies designed around one way of doing research can entrench that way of doing it, along with the inequities that come with it, and push other kinds of research to the margins.

NEXT SLIDE

Three kinds of reproducibility

Even research that aims to be reproducible means different things by the word.

Steven Goodman, Daniele Fanelli and John Ioannidis distinguish three kinds.

Methods reproducibility means repeating the same procedures on the same data and getting the same result.

CLICK

Results reproducibility means running a new study and getting results that corroborate the original.

CLICK

Inferential reproducibility means drawing conclusions of similar strength from a replication or a reanalysis, and that comes down to expert judgement.

Open data and open code serve the first kind most directly.

They do much less for the other two, which depend on new studies and on judgement.

CLICK

Which of these makes sense for the kind of research you know best?

And is there one that doesn’t apply at all?

Give thirty seconds, then take two or three answers

Running the question. Allow about two minutes.

  1. Once the question appears, give the room thirty seconds to think or to talk with a neighbour.
  2. Ask two or three people to name a field or a kind of research they know, and say which kind of reproducibility fits it.
  3. Expect lab sciences and quantitative social science to pick methods or results reproducibility. Expect qualitative, historical and archival work to say that none of the three fits well. That answer is Leonelli’s point, so say so.
  4. If nobody answers, offer an oral history archive. Nobody can rerun an interview, and a second interviewer would hear a different story. Ask which kind of reproducibility could apply.

5 Tensions with AI

AI puts new pressure on the access terms and the maintenance problems we just looked at, and it changes who might do curation work.

NEXT SLIDE

Open licences and AI training

Open science wants research outputs to be reusable by anyone, for any purpose.

The licences it relies on, especially Creative Commons Attribution, were designed for exactly that.

CLICK

They were written before large language models, and they give authors no practical way to say yes to a student and no to a company training a commercial model.

Creative Commons started a project in 2025 called CC signals, which would let people state their preferences about AI reuse in a form both people and machines can read.

Those signals only work if AI developers choose to respect them.

For curators, this reopens the question of access terms.

When the reuser is a crawler feeding a training pipeline, it’s no longer obvious what open access is meant to accomplish.

NEXT SLIDE

AI crawlers and open repositories

AI also strains the infrastructure that open science depends on.

In 2025 the Confederation of Open Access Repositories, an international association of repositories, surveyed sixty-six of them around the world.

CLICK

More than ninety percent said aggressive bots were hitting their services, usually more than once a week.

Respondents described slowdowns, outages and crashes that needed manual restarts, and they said the traffic had grown sharply over the previous two years.

CLICK

Repositories have responded by blocking or throttling bots.

Some of these measures work, but they also block legitimate users and the benign services, like search indexers, that make content findable in the first place.

This is the maintenance problem from earlier, made worse.

Repositories built to give people access now spend staff time and money keeping machines out.

NEXT SLIDE

Who should curate?

AI tools are now being pitched as a fix for the curation burden.

They draft metadata, suggest subject terms and summarize datasets.

So let’s sort some jobs.

For each one, tell me who should do it, whether that’s the researcher, a curator, a model, or no one at all.

One vote per job.

Run the sort, then come back for the debrief

A model can draft a description, but it doesn’t know how the data were made, and nobody can hold it accountable for what it writes.

Someone still has to check the output and sign off on it.

Setting access terms depends on consent and on the people the data describe, and a model has no way of knowing either.

Checking a deposit is exactly the kind of judgement curators are trained for.

And keeping files usable for ten years tends to end up with no one, unless an institution takes it on.

Running the sort. Allow about five minutes.

  1. Read the first job aloud. Ask for hands for researcher, then curator, then model, then no one, one vote per person. Say the leading answer aloud.
  2. Repeat for the other three jobs at a brisk pace, about forty-five seconds each.
  3. Go back to the job with the most even split and ask one person from each side to explain.
  4. Expect describing the data to split between researcher and model, and keeping files usable to land on curator. If it does, ask which curator, at which institution, and paid by whom.
  5. If the room goes quiet, ask who has written a metadata record and how long it took.
  6. Finish with the debrief paragraph above.

NEXT SLIDE once the activity wraps up

Open science needs curation

Open science has good motivations, and Canada is putting serious policy behind it.

But most of the conversation about open science is about releasing things.

Curation asks what happens next.

Someone has to keep data meaningful and available, for particular people, over a long stretch of time.

Right now a lot of that work falls to researchers who were never trained for it and are rarely paid for it, supported by curators and repositories whose own funding is uncertain.

Look back at the words you gave me at the start.

I suspect most of them were about access, and very few were about upkeep.

Upkeep is the part of open science your field knows best, and it’s where people with your training are most needed.

Discussion

Running the discussion. Leave this slide up through questions. Take questions from the room first. If things go quiet, read one card aloud and let someone answer, or ask the room to pick a card. The notes below give background for each.

  1. Grants end, and the proposed Tri-Agency requirement asks for ten years of retention. The options include research budgets, institutional libraries, national infrastructure like the Alliance and Borealis, and disciplinary repositories. Each one shifts the work onto different people, and each one can disappear when its own funding changes.

  2. Researchers now write data management plans, choose licences and pick repositories, often with no training. Libraries and data centres hold most of the relevant expertise. It’s worth asking what a first-year graduate student actually needs to know, and who is in a position to teach it.

  3. Open licences permit broad reuse, and many researchers are uneasy about their work training commercial models. Restricting access would cut against open science mandates, and leaving it open feeds crawlers that already strain repositories. Preference signals depend on AI developers respecting them.
